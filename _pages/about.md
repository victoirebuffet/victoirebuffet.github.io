Studying climate was never the obvious path—but looking back, it makes perfect sense. After high school, I double-majored in science and music in Paris. Science kept my parents happy; music was my real focus. I was where I could expand my network, immerse myself in the scene, and work toward a career in music.  
I’d always been fascinated by how things work—especially physics—but science was something I studied, not something I pursued. Music took priority, and eventually, I achieved my goal: making a living playing guitar. Then COVID hit, and suddenly the world was split into “essential” and “non-essential.” That distinction made me rethink what I wanted to contribute. My passion for nature, animals, and the environment had always been there, but for the first time, I felt the need to do more than just appreciate them. So, while I never put the guitar down, I returned to science—this time with purpose.  
Now, as a PhD researcher, I study Atmospheric Rivers—intense moisture transport systems that shape Antarctica’s climate. These systems play a key role in the continent’s mass balance, while also influencing global atmospheric circulation, ice sheet stability, and sea level rise. By refining our understanding of these processes, I aim to improve climate projections and contribute to a better understanding of Antarctic climate variability.  
Outside of research, I enjoy learning new things, spending time outdoors, strength training, and writing music. I also share my workspace with Kinaï, my canine meeting disrupter, and Yoko, my feline assistant, who takes her role as a keyboard napper quite seriously.
